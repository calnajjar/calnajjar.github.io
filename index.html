About

I study how people decide when probabilities are unknown and when their payoffs depend on others. I build models of these choices and test them by how well they predict behavior they were not fitted to.

My work develops dispersion-based models of ambiguity attitudes and other-regarding preferences, and compares them with leading benchmarks such as cumulative prospect theory and the Nash equilibrium in out-of-sample tests on incentivized experimental data. I estimate the models by maximum likelihood and publish my data and code. I hold an M.S. in Computer Engineering from Texas A&M University, where I was a Fulbright Scholar, and a B.S. in Electrical Engineering from the University of Jordan. I expect to complete my Ph.D. in May 2027.

Job market paper

Game Geometric Dispersion Model with Comparative Out-of-Sample Empirical Study in Other-Regarding Preferences

with Behnam Malakooti · Second-round revision, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics: Systems

The Nash equilibrium assumes that players care only about their own payoffs, yet experiments show that opponents' payoffs also shape choices. Existing other-regarding models are either linear in payoff differences or impose a single nonlinear form whether a player is ahead or behind. We propose the Game-Geometric Dispersion (G-GD) model, which applies separate nonlinear adjustments to the advantaged and disadvantaged branches based on the geometric dispersion of all players' payoffs. With two parameters, G-GD represents self-interest and four types of other-regarding preferences, and it adds negligible cost to computing the equilibrium. Using 80 observed choice distributions in three datasets from four published experiments, we compare G-GD with the Nash equilibrium and three leading other-regarding models in repeated out-of-sample tests. G-GD attains the highest mean out-of-sample log-likelihood in all three datasets, and its advantage is significant at the 5% level. Estimated preference types differ by game category, suggesting that other-regarding behavior depends on strategic context.
